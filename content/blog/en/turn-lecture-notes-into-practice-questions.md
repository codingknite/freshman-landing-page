This semester my friends and I were determined. We walked into the lecture hall, sat through the lecture, took the notes, and even colour-coded them. Now exam season's arrived and we realise the notes have done nothing except sit there. The reason is simple: our notes are a record of information, not a test of it. To actually remember what's in them, we need to turn them into questions we have to answer from memory.

If you're facing the same predicament, this guide walks you through exactly how to do that: the manual method, the mistakes that make practice questions useless, and a faster way to do it when you have too much material and too little time.

## Why Practice Questions Beat Re-Reading Your Notes

Re-reading feels productive because the material looks familiar. However, familiarity isn't the same as being able to recall something on a blank exam page. Practice questions force retrieval, which is the process of pulling information out of memory, and retrieval is what strengthens it. We break down the science of this in our guide to [active recall vs. re-reading](/en/blog/active-recall-vs-rereading).

The catch, though, is that nobody hands you a set of questions for your own lectures, so you usually have to build them. Here's how.

## 1: Clean Up Your Notes First

Before you write a single question, skim your notes within 24 hours of the lecture while it's still fresh. Fill in gaps, fix anything you scribbled too fast to read, and mark the parts you didn't fully understand. Questions built on confused notes just give you confusing questions.

## 2: Find the Testable Ideas

Not every line in your notes deserves a question. Look for:

- **Definitions and key terms** that the lecturer emphasised or repeated
- **Processes and sequences**, such as steps, stages, or cause-and-effect chains
- **Comparisons**, meaning anything the lecturer contrasted (X vs. Y)
- **Examples and cases** that were used to illustrate a principle
- **Anything the lecturer said "will be on the exam"**, or anything that appears in past papers

A good rule of thumb: if you could imagine it as an exam question, it deserves one here.

## 3: Turn Each Idea into a Question

The format of your question matters more than most students realise. Here are the types that work best:

- **Direct recall:** "What are the three stages of memory processing?"
- **Explain-it questions:** "Why does spaced practice improve retention more than cramming?"
- **Compare and contrast:** "How does classical conditioning differ from operant conditioning?"
- **Application questions:** "A patient presents with X. Which condition is most likely, and why?"
- **Fill in the blank:** "The _____ is responsible for regulating blood sugar."

Mix these types. Direct recall tests whether you know the facts; explain-it and application questions test whether you actually understand them, which is what most university exams care about.

## 4: Write the Answer Separately

Write the question on one side and the answer on the other, or in a separate document. The key rule: **never keep the answer visible while you practice.** The moment you can see the answer, you're back to recognition instead of recall.

## 5: Practice Out Loud or on Paper

Answer each question from memory before checking. Struggling for a few seconds before you find the answer is not a sign something's wrong. That effort is what makes the memory stick. Mark each question as easy, medium, or hard based on how it went.

## 6: Space Out Your Review

Don't answer all your questions once and call it done. Come back to the hard ones the next day, then a few days later, then a week later. This is spaced repetition, and it's what turns short-term familiarity into long-term memory. Easy questions can wait longer between reviews; hard ones need to come back sooner.

## Common Mistakes That Make Practice Questions Useless

**Writing questions that are too vague.** "What is memory?" is almost impossible to answer well. "What's the difference between short-term and long-term memory?" gives you something specific to retrieve.

**Copying sentences straight from your notes.** If your question is just a note with a blank in it, you may be matching word patterns instead of understanding the idea.

**Only making easy questions.** It's tempting to write questions you already know the answers to. Deliberately include the topics you find confusing.

**Never testing across topics.** Real exams mix material. Once you've done each topic separately, shuffle questions together so you practice recognising which concept applies.

Although all these tips and tricks are effective, here's the honest reality of everything above: doing it well takes hours. One lecture might produce 15 to 30 good questions. Multiply that by every lecture in every course across a semester, and manually building a question bank becomes a part-time job. This is exactly why so many students understand the method but never keep it up.
