You study a topic for two hours, feel like you've nailed it, and a week later it's mostly gone. That isn't a personal failing. It's how memory works, and spaced repetition is the method built to work with it instead of against it.

This guide explains what spaced repetition is, why it works, and how to build it into your study routine, even if you've never used a flashcard app.

## What Is Spaced Repetition?

Spaced repetition is a study method where you review material at increasing intervals over time, instead of all at once. You might review a topic the day after you learn it, then three days later, then a week later, then two weeks later. Each time you successfully recall it, the gap before the next review gets longer.

The opposite is cramming: reviewing everything in one long session right before an exam. Cramming can get you through a test tomorrow, but the material tends to disappear soon after, because it was never given the chance to settle into long-term memory.

## Why It Works: The Forgetting Curve

In the 1880s, the psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus ran memory experiments on himself and found that we forget newly learned information quickly at first, then more slowly over time. This pattern is known as the **forgetting curve**.

The important discovery is what happens when you review. Each time you retrieve a memory just as it starts to fade, it becomes stronger and fades more slowly afterward. So the best moment to review something is not right after you learn it, when it's still fresh and review feels easy. It's shortly before you'd forget it, when recalling it takes real effort.

Researchers call the broader finding the **spacing effect**: studying the same material across several sessions produces better long-term retention than studying it the same total amount in one sitting. It's one of the most consistently supported findings in the science of learning.

## Spaced Repetition vs. Active Recall

These two ideas are often confused, but they answer different questions.

**Active recall** is *how* you review: testing yourself from memory instead of re-reading. (We cover this in [active recall vs. re-reading](/en/blog/active-recall-vs-rereading).)

**Spaced repetition** is *when* you review: spreading those tests out over time.

They work best together. Active recall makes each review session effective, and spacing makes the benefits last. Re-reading your notes on a spaced schedule is better than cramming, but answering questions on a spaced schedule is far better still.

## A Simple Spaced Repetition Schedule

You don't need software to start. A common pattern for reviewing a topic after you first learn it looks like this:

- **Day 1:** review the day after you first study it
- **Day 3:** review again
- **Day 7:** one week after learning
- **Day 14:** two weeks after learning
- **Day 30:** one month after learning

If your exam is sooner than 30 days away, fit as many of these as you can and add a final review the day before.

The exact numbers aren't magic. The principle is what matters: gaps that grow as the memory gets stronger.

### Not Every Topic Needs the Same Schedule

A fixed schedule is a good starting point, but real material isn't equal. Some concepts click right away and some keep slipping. A better approach adjusts to how you actually perform:

- If you answer a question easily, wait longer before seeing it again.
- If you struggle or get it wrong, bring it back sooner.

This is the idea behind spaced repetition software. It tracks how well you know each item and schedules the next review for you, so your time goes to the material you're most likely to forget instead of what you already know well.

## How to Start Using Spaced Repetition Today

1. **Turn your notes into questions.** Spaced repetition only works if you have something to review. Convert your material into questions you can answer from memory. Our guide on [how to turn lecture notes into practice questions](/en/blog/turn-lecture-notes-into-practice-questions) walks through it step by step.
2. **Start early.** Spacing needs time. Beginning three weeks before an exam gives you far more useful review sessions than beginning three days before.
3. **Keep sessions short.** Fifteen to twenty minutes a day beats a long weekly session. Short, frequent reviews are easier to keep up, and consistency is what makes spacing work.
4. **Mark what's hard.** After each question, note whether it was easy, medium, or hard, and bring the hard ones back sooner.
5. **Mix topics.** Once you've reviewed individual topics, shuffle them together. Real exams don't tell you which chapter a question comes from.

## Common Mistakes

**Reviewing too soon or too often.** If recalling something feels effortless every time, you're probably reviewing it too frequently. A bit of struggle is a good sign.

**Skipping days and then catching up in one block.** That turns spaced review back into cramming. If you miss a day, simply carry on from where you are.

**Reviewing by re-reading.** Spacing helps re-reading a little. It helps testing yourself much more.

**Giving up on the setup.** Most people quit because building and scheduling questions by hand takes so much effort, not because the method fails.
