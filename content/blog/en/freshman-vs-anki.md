If you've spent any time researching how to study more effectively, you've run into Anki. It's the app pre-med students swear by, with a cult following on Reddit, and for over a decade it's been the closest thing to a gold standard for spaced repetition. So why are students increasingly switching to AI-native tools like Freshman instead?

The honest answer is that Anki and Freshman are solving the same problem (passive re-reading doesn't work and active recall does), but they were built for very different kinds of students, in very different eras of technology. Here's an honest breakdown of where each one wins.

## What Anki Actually Is

Anki is a free, open-source flashcard program built around spaced repetition. You review cards on a schedule that adapts to how well you remember them. It's been around since 2010, it's endlessly customizable, and it has a massive community sharing pre-made decks for everything from anatomy to organic chemistry.

The catch, though, is that Anki gives you the engine, not the fuel. It doesn't create your flashcards for you. You either build every card by hand, card by card, or you download someone else's deck and hope it matches your syllabus. For students with hours to spend formatting cards, or those willing to live inside someone else's pre-made deck, that trade-off has always seemed worth it. For everyone else, the setup time is the single biggest reason people quit before the habit sticks.

There's also a platform quirk worth knowing before you commit: Anki's desktop version and AnkiDroid (Android) are free, but the official iOS app is a one-time $24.99 purchase. That funds the project's development, but it does mean the "free flashcard app" reputation doesn't fully apply if you're on iPhone.

## What Freshman Does Differently

Freshman starts from a different assumption: your notes already exist. You've already sat through the lecture, already read the chapter, already have the PDF. So instead of asking you to convert that material into flashcards manually, Freshman does it for you! All you need to do is upload your notes, textbook chapters, PDFs or past papers, and it automatically generates calibrated active recall questions from them.

From there, the two approaches converge again. Freshman delivers a personalized daily revision feed, adapting to what you consistently get right or wrong. That's the same spaced-repetition principle Anki is built on, just without requiring you to build or maintain the deck yourself. It also goes a step further than most flashcard tools by turning old exams into interactive, auto-graded practice tests, not just static PDFs to re-read.

## Side-by-Side: Where Each One Wins

### Getting started

Anki has a real learning curve: new users often spend their first few sessions watching tutorials just to feel comfortable with the interface and settings. Freshman is built to go from upload to your first test in minutes, with no configuration required.

### Card creation

Anki requires manual card creation, or relies on decks other people built for their own courses, which may not match what you're actually being taught. Freshman generates questions directly from your own material, so what you're tested on always matches what you're responsible for.

### Customization

This is Anki's home turf. Its add-on ecosystem and deep configuration options let power users tune scheduling algorithms, card templates and study logic to an extreme degree. Freshman deliberately trades that depth of customization for speed and simplicity: there is very little to configure because there is very little you need to change.

### Content types

Anki is flashcard-first. Freshman supports several distinct test modes, including quizzes, active recall and interactive past papers, so a single upload can be studied multiple ways depending on what stage of exam prep you're in.

### Cost structure

Anki's desktop and Android apps are free; iOS requires a one-time payment. Freshman is built as a mobile-first daily habit tool, with its own pricing structure to match that use case.

### Community and longevity

Anki has a 15-year head start, a massive shared-deck library and a devoted community. If you want to study from decks other people have already built, nothing beats it. Freshman's strength isn't community decks; it's turning your own material into a test bank without needing anyone else's.

## So Which One Should You Actually Use?

If you already enjoy the process of building and tweaking your own flashcard system, and you're studying a subject where great community decks already exist, Anki remains an excellent, genuinely free (on desktop) option, and it has earned its reputation.

If your bottleneck has always been the setup (staring at a blank deck, dreading the hours of manual card creation, or abandoning the habit before it sticks), that's exactly the gap Freshman was built to close. Upload what you already have, and start actively recalling it the same day instead of spending your first week just building the tool to study with.

Both apps are built on the same well-established science. The real question isn't which method works; it's which one you'll actually stick with. For a lot of students, the app that removes the setup wins simply because they use it every day instead of a few times before giving up.
